Abstract writing with Intro technique we taught
Same as the intro (for the abstract), except the intro dedicated an entire paragraph to the 6 bullet points you have, abstract does 1-2 sentences instead:
1. Problem motivation: Explain the main problem and why it matters, optionally why it’s not been solved.
2. Set up the bit/assumption: Identify the assumption in prior work you're challenging/inverting.
3. Flip the bit (=contribution): Introduce your novel idea/contribution that challenges/inverts the assumption of prior work.
4. Instantiate the bit flip (=contribution/your solution): Describe how your solution concretely applies the new idea.
5. Evaluation: Briefly mention your method for evaluating the solution.
6. Implications: Discuss the broader impact and significance of your findings.

Slide 1: The Strategy & Theory (The "Why")
Purpose: To connect your real-world VeriBench-DT example back to the core principles you are teaching in your presentation.
It introduces the mental model your students need to adopt: writing for the "30-second scan" because the title and abstract might be all someone actually reads.
It explicitly grounds the example in your "Bit Flip" concept —shifting them away from the trap of writing a slow, multi-sentence intro, and towards delivering an immediate, surprising hook.
It invokes the "Feynman Test" to emphasize why this strategy matters: if the reader doesn't learn something new immediately, they won't decide to read on.

\begin{abstract}
Current autoformalization systems for code often successfully prove theorems that are semantically disconnected from the source code they intend to verify.
We introduce \textsc{VeriBench-DT}, a benchmark that enforces end-to-end grounding by validating agent-generated Lean~4 formalizations against original Python programs via differential testing.
Unlike existing benchmarks that rely on static translation checks or LLM-based judges, \textsc{VeriBench-DT} executes both the source (Python) and the target (Lean) on shared inputs to verify behavioral equivalence.
We contribute a curated dataset of $N$ Python--Lean pairs and a novel multiplicative evaluation metric that exposes \emph{verification hallucinations}---cases where agents produce correct proofs for incorrect specifications.
Evaluation of state-of-the-art agents reveals a critical gap: while models achieve $X$\% success on formal proof generation, their end-to-end reliability drops to $Y$\% when grounded against original code execution.
\textsc{VeriBench-DT} provides the necessary tooling to measure and close this gap, moving autoformalization from isolated theorem proving to trustworthy software verification.
\end{abstract}
