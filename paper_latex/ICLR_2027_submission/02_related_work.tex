\section{Related Work}
\paragraph{LLM-as-a-judge validation.}
\textsc{G-Eval}~\citep{Liu2023GEval} and \textsc{MT-Bench}~\citep{Zheng2023MTBench} compare judges with human ratings, leaving reliability dependent on the evaluated tasks and configurations.
Bavaresco et al.~\citep{Kocmi2024HumanVsLLM} document this dependence across tasks rather than establishing a universal judge.
Schroeder and Wood-Doughty~\citep{Li2024TrustLLMJudgments} measure consistency across repeated judgments, which does not by itself establish correctness.
Our protocol retains human agreement as a validation criterion and adds tests with specified directions of response.

\paragraph{Judges for code.}
\textsc{ICE-Score}~\citep{Zhuo2024ICEScore} and \textsc{CodeJudge}~\citep{TongZhang2024CodeJudge} use models to assess generated code where execution tests provide incomplete evidence, but their human-agreement results do not certify later judge configurations.
\textsc{SWT-Bench} validates generated tests against developers' fixes~\citep{mundler2024swtbench}, requiring a reference behavior rather than treating test execution as proof of test validity.
Our controlled perturbations serve a related purpose for judges: they specify how a score should change without labeling each perturbed artifact.

\paragraph{Judging formal statements.}
\textsc{FormalAlign} learns alignment between informal and formal statements~\citep{lu2024formalalign}, whereas our target is the behavior of a judge comparing candidate and gold specifications.
\textsc{ReForm} validates formal-statement judges against expert annotations~\citep{chen2026reform}, without establishing that later configurations satisfy our behavioral properties.
\textsc{VeriBench} evaluates agent-generated Lean artifacts~\citep{miranda2026veribench}; we study the judges used to score their formal specifications.
These tasks require checking what a statement means, beyond whether a proof checker accepts it.
