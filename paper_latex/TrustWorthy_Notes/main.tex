\paragraph{Notation note.}
\(\hat p_T\) denotes the target-judge pdf/pmf; the hat indicates that the estimate is finite-sample.
We write \(\hat p_T\) (or \(\hat p_H\) when the target is human) and \(\hat p_{AJ}\) for the AI-judge side.

\section{Behaviors and agents}

\begin{definition}[Universally trustworthy AI judge]
\label{def:tw}
Let \(B\) be a set of benchmarks and \(A\) a set of agents.
An AI judge \(aj \in AJ\) is universally trustworthy if, for every benchmark \(b \in B\) and every agent/solver \(a \in A\),
\(aj\) distributionally matches the corresponding \emph{target judge} (\(tj \in TJ\))
(e.g., a human, a persona, a strict rubric, a lenient rubric, etc.):
\[
\forall b \in B,\; \forall a \in A,\quad
\text{if } t_j = a_j
\quad\Rightarrow\quad
\hat p_{AJ}\bigl(s \mid x,\, \hat{y}(c),\, y^*,\, \theta_j\bigr)
\;=\;
\hat p_T\bigl(s \mid x,\, \hat{y}(c),\, y^*,\, \theta_* + t_j\bigr),
\]
where equality may be measured by KL divergence (or total variation).
\end{definition}

\paragraph{Remark.}
The precondition \(t_j = a_j\) (persona / target match) is required.
If, for example, we set \(t_j = \text{strict}\) but the judge \(a_j\) has parameters/configuration
\(\theta_{a_j}\) (prompt, weights, etc.) that map to a \emph{lenient} judge,
then the definition is unsatisfiable.

\paragraph{Remark (preference vs.\ trustworthiness).}
The literature typically uses ``trustworthiness'' implicitly to mean matching humans
(human judgments; sometimes loosely ``human preferences'').
That is not RLHF preference (which of two answers to prefer).
Preference can also denote the objective itself, so the two notions are close:
preference is the more general term and can include trustworthiness.
Once a frame of reference is fixed (objective or not---e.g.\ a persona), the distinction collapses;
there is no residual gap between ``I do not trust it'' and ``I do not prefer it.''

\section{Practical instantiations}

\begin{definition}[Common practice (\(m_1\))]
Most work treats an AI judge as trustworthy when its scores correlate with human scores on a panel:
\[
\TI_{m_1}(aj) \;=\; \mathrm{Corr}\bigl(\hat s_{aj},\, \hat s_T\bigr).
\]
This is a correlational match (dependence between the two score vectors), not a full distributional match (\(m_0\)) and not a pure first-moment match.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Valid-and-stable (\(m_2\) / \(\TI_{2'}\))]
A stricter practical criterion combines validity (correlation) with stability (low repeat variance) via a geometric mean:
\[
\TI_{2'}(aj) \;=\; \Bigl(\mathrm{Corr}\bigl(\hat s_{aj},\, \hat s_T\bigr) \cdot \bigl(1 - \mathrm{std}(\hat s_{aj})\bigr)\Bigr)^{1/2}.
\]
This is the working definition of trustworthiness\({}_{2'}\) used in these notes.
\end{definition}

\paragraph{Remark (ideal vs.\ practical).}
The ideal target is full distributional match of scores once a persona is fixed (Definition~\ref{def:tw}).
In practice this is expensive: many humans and many repeated scores would be required.
We therefore approximate the ideal by matching a low-order moment (correlation of scores) and requiring the judge itself to be stable (low repeat standard deviation).
These two quantities are combined by geometric mean to obtain \(\TI_{2'}\).
The practical criterion only captures limited moments of the ideal match.
