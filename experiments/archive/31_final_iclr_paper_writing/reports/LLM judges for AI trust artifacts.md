# The abstract's opener overclaims on tests

**The first sentence of the ICLR 2027 abstract ("LLM judges are increasingly central to evaluating AI-generated code, tests, and formal specifications") is half right.** The general premise holds, and so does the claim for formal theorem statements. The claim overstates code and is wrong about tests. LLM judges are now a standard evaluation instrument: MT-Bench coined the term at NeurIPS 2023, three dedicated surveys appeared within weeks of each other in late 2024, and a 2026 keyword study counts 650 ACL Anthology papers on the topic. For Lean theorem statements, open prover pipelines use LLM judges to filter corpora of up to **1.78M autoformalized statements**, and ReForm (ICLR 2026) writes that recent work "predominantly rel[ies] on LLMs as judges." For code, "increasingly" holds but "central" does not. Execution still decides headline benchmarks and RL rewards, and judges are central only in code review, in grading where no tests exist, and in choosing among candidate solutions. For tests, the sentence fails. None of the flagship test-generation benchmarks the researchers checked uses an LLM judge. They grade by execution: compilation, coverage, mutation score, and whether an issue's reproduction test goes from failing to passing (fail-to-pass). LLM judges appear there only as triage filters inside Meta's pipelines, or as the subject of studies that find them random and sensitive to response order. The paper's deeper motivation is the question of who checks the tests, specifications and theorems that AI now writes. The literature supports it in pieces but never states it whole, so the paper can present the unified framing as an emerging problem. The recommended replacement is: *"LLM judges are increasingly used to evaluate AI-generated code and formal specifications, especially where passing tests or type-checking cannot show that an artifact captures its intended meaning."* Separately, five `refs.bib` entries get authors or titles wrong.

## LLM judges became a default evaluation instrument within three years

The general half of the claim will survive any reviewer. Zheng et al. named "LLM-as-a-judge" with MT-Bench and showed that GPT-4 judges reach "over 80% agreement" with human preferences, "the same level of agreement between humans" ([Zheng et al., NeurIPS 2023 D&B](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.05685)). The paper has about **11,349 Semantic Scholar citations**. Three dedicated surveys appeared within roughly three weeks of each other in November–December 2024 ([Gu et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.15594); [D. Li et al., EMNLP 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.16594); [H. Li et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.05579)), which marks the practice as a recognized subfield. The standard open-ended leaderboards all rely on a judge and validate it against human Elo ratings from Chatbot Arena. Arena-Hard-Auto reports **98.6% correlation with human preference rankings "at a cost of $20"** ([T. Li et al., ICML 2025 per S2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.11939)). WildBench's WB-Reward reaches Pearson 0.98 ([Lin et al., ICLR 2025 per S2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.04770)). Length control lifts AlpacaEval's Spearman correlation from 0.94 to 0.98 ([Dubois et al., COLM 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.04475)). By 2025, frontier labs used judges to grade correctness and expert rubrics, not just chat quality. Humanity's Last Exam uses o3-mini "as a judge to verify answer correctness" ([Phan et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.14249)). HealthBench grades **48,562 physician-written rubric criteria** with GPT-4.1 ([Arora et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.08775)). PaperBench grades agent-written replication code with an LLM judge that has its own validation benchmark ([Starace et al., ICML 2025 per S2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.01848)). Judges also shape training. RLAIF matches RLHF on summarization and dialogue ([Lee et al., ICML 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.00267)). Self-Rewarding models judge their own outputs to produce rewards ([Yuan et al., ICML 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.10020)). DeepSeek-R1 trains its helpfulness reward model on DeepSeek-V3 pairwise judgments ([DeepSeek-AI](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.12948)).

Hard adoption *rates* do not exist in the literature; the citable numbers are counts. Doğruöz et al. find **650 ACL Anthology papers** mentioning LLM-as-a-judge and call it "the dominant evaluation paradigm for many natural language generation tasks" ([Doğruöz et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.02235)). That study is a fresh preprint, and the count is a keyword match with no denominator. The researchers' Semantic Scholar exact-phrase counts rise from **5 papers (2023) to 120 (2024), 645 (2025) and 913 (2026 through September 22)** ([Semantic Scholar API](https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/search/bulk?query=%22LLM-as-a-judge%22&year=2025&fields=title)). These are noisy keyword matches: they justify "increasingly" but are not usage rates. A vendor survey of 1,340 respondents reports that 53.3% use LLM-as-judge, though it is ambiguous whether the denominator is all respondents or only teams that run evals ([LangChain](https://www.langchain.com/state-of-agent-engineering)). The safest peer-reviewed adoption statements for the intro come from JudgeBench, where judges "are increasingly used to assess, compare, and improve models" ([Tan et al., ICLR 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.12784)), and from Bavaresco et al., who note "an increasing trend towards evaluating NLP models with LLMs instead of human judgments" ([Bavaresco et al., ACL 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.18403)).

The same literature supports the abstract's *second* sentence. Bavaresco et al. conclude that LLMs "should be carefully validated against human judgments before being used as evaluators." Their agreement with humans varies with the property judged, the expertise of the human raters, and whether the text was written by a human or a model. Judges fail where correctness matters most. JudgeBench finds GPT-4o "just slightly better than random guessing" on hard knowledge, reasoning, math and coding pairs. Without a correct reference, judges agree with experts "only on questions the judges were able to correctly answer themselves" ([Krumdick et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.05061), venue UNVERIFIED). Suppose a judge is no more accurate than the model it evaluates. Then no debiasing method can cut the number of ground-truth labels needed by more than half ([Dorner, Nastl, Hardt, ICLR 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.13341)). And a "null model" that always returns the same output reaches an **86.5% length-controlled win rate on AlpacaEval 2.0** ([X. Zheng et al., ICLR 2025 Oral](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.07137)). Taken together, these results show that checking agreement with humans is necessary but does not scale. That is the premise the paper's certification protocol builds on.

## Code judges grew beside execution, not in place of it

Work on LLM judges for code is growing fast. He et al. review **42 primary studies** of LLM-as-a-judge in software engineering. The count grew from 1 publication in 2023 to 15 in 2024, and to 26 in 2025 through August alone ([He et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.24367)). The March 2025 version of the review covered 16. On the methods side, ICE-Score has an LLM score code without tests or references ([Zhuo, Findings of EACL 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.14317)). CodeJudge evaluates "semantic correctness of generated code without the need for test cases" ([Tong & Zhang, EMNLP 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.02184)). Against human scores, an ISSTA 2025 study finds that output-based judges reach **Pearson 81.32 on translation and 68.51 on generation**, versus 34.23 and 64.92 for the reference-based ChrF++ metric ([R. Wang et al., ISSTA 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.06193)). Reliability is still poor. Across 1,405 Java methods and 1,281 Python functions, "even the best-performing LLM frequently misjudges the correctness of the code" ([Crupi et al., IEEE TSE 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.16587)). On CodeJudgeBench, 26 judge models "still exhibit significant randomness," and swapping the order of the two responses "can substantially impact accuracy" ([Jiang et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.10535); S2 lists ACL, but the track and year are UNVERIFIED). Every tested judge is swayed by variable names, comments and formatting ([Moon et al., Findings of EACL 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.16222)). That last result is the closest external evidence for the paper's alpha-renaming invariance property.

**Judges are genuinely central in code review.** GitHub now lets admins authorize Copilot to sign off on pull-request approval. The feature is off by default and in public preview ([GitHub changelog, 2026-09-01](https://github.blog/changelog/2026-09-01-copilot-code-review-can-now-approve-pull-requests/)). In March 2026 Anthropic launched a multi-agent Code Review for Claude Code, at an estimated $15–25 per review. It was built explicitly to cope with the flood of AI-written pull requests ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/03/09/anthropic-launches-code-review-tool-to-check-flood-of-ai-generated-code/)). Google's AutoCommenter, an LLM-backed code-review system, was deployed to "tens of thousands of developers" ([Vijayvergiya et al., AIware '24](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.13565)). An industrial study found that **73.8% of automated review comments were resolved**, although average PR closure time rose from 5h52m to 8h20m ([Cihan et al., ICSE-SEIP 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.18531)). Judges also grade tasks that have no tests: PaperBench, and DevAI's 365 hierarchical requirements ([Zhuge et al., ICML 2025 per S2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.10934)). They also pick among candidate agent solutions. The SWE-RM reward model, which scores patches without running them, lifts Qwen3-Coder-Max from **67.0% to 74.6% on SWE-Bench Verified** by selecting among samples at test time ([Shum et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.21919)).

**Judges are not central for benchmarks or RL rewards.** SWE-bench ([Jimenez et al., ICLR 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.06770)), EvalPlus, BigCodeBench and LiveCodeBench all grade by running tests. DeepSeek-R1 scores code against test cases. It explicitly avoided neural reward models because they "are susceptible to reward hacking during large-scale reinforcement learning" ([DeepSeek-AI](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.12948)). R2E-Gym finds that verifiers which never execute the code "are biased and often rely on stylistic features." Test-based and execution-free verifiers each plateau around 42–43% on their own, while a hybrid of the two reaches 51% ([Jain et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.07164), venue UNVERIFIED). The accurate picture is that LLM verifiers complement execution; they do not replace it.

Judges are needed at all because passing tests does not mean correct code. Expanding HumanEval's tests 80-fold cuts pass@k by up to 19.3–28.9% ([Liu et al., EvalPlus, NeurIPS 2023 per S2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.01210)). And **29.6% of plausible SWE-bench patches behave differently from the ground-truth patch** ([Wang, Pradel, Liu, ICSE 2026 per S2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.15223)). This is the paper's bridge to Lean: a theorem statement that type-checks is the analogue of a patch that passes its tests.

## Execution, not LLM judges, grades AI-written tests

The field measures test quality by running the tests. The metrics are compile or build rate, pass rate, coverage, mutation score (the share of seeded faults the tests catch) and fail-to-pass issue reproduction. Meta's TestGen-LLM kept only tests that built (75%), passed reliably (57%) and raised coverage (25%). Engineers then accepted **73% of its recommendations** ([Alshahwan et al., FSE 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.09171)). TestGenEval reports pass@1, all-pass@1, coverage and mutation score, and uses no LLM judge. GPT-4o reaches only 35.2% average coverage on it ([Jain, Synnaeve, Rozière](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.00752), venue UNVERIFIED). SWT-Bench scores issue-reproduction rate and coverage change ([Mündler et al., NeurIPS 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.12952)). TestEval scores coverage ([Wang et al., NAACL 2025 Findings](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.04531)). MuTAP switches to mutation score because "coverage is weakly correlated with the efficiency of tests in bug detection" ([Dakhel et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.16557), venue UNVERIFIED). None of the flagship test-generation benchmarks the researchers examined uses an LLM judge as a metric.

LLM judges appear only at the margins, and where they have been measured, they perform poorly. In Meta's just-in-time catching-test generation, "rule-based and LLM-based assessors" together "reduce human review load by 70%." Engineers still confirmed the **8 true positives among 41 reported catches** ([Becker et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.22832), FSE 2026 industry submission). Meta's ACH pipeline uses an LLM to spot equivalent mutants, reaching precision 0.79 and recall 0.47 ([Foster et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.12862), acceptance UNVERIFIED). The benchmark studies are negative. CodeJudgeBench includes a unit-test-generation task, and its judges show "significant randomness" ([Jiang et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.10535)). "Bias in the Loop" finds judge decisions on code generation, code repair and test generation "highly sensitive to prompt biases even when the underlying code snippet is unchanged." The effect is large enough to "alter relative model rankings" ([Zhao, Esmaeili, Fard 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.16790)). And "LLMs are better at generating test oracles rather than classifying the correct ones" ([Konstantinou, Degiovanni, Papadakis](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.21136)). The main SE judge-alignment study (ISSTA 2025) has no test task at all. The well-supported direction is the reverse one, where tests judge code. Examples are CodeT ([Chen et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.10397)), UTGen ([Prasad et al., COLM 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.01619)) and CURE ([Wang et al., NeurIPS 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.03136)); SWT-Bench's generated tests also double SWE-Agent's precision as a filter on its patches.

**AI-written tests are nonetheless a real and well-documented trust problem.** Agents write tests at scale. The AIDev dataset spans **456,000 PRs by five coding agents across 61,000 repositories** ([Li, Zhang, Hassan 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.15003)). Agent PRs that contain tests "are more common over time" ([Haque, Ingale, Csallner 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.03556)). Agents include test changes in 49.6% of PRs that touch code under test. Their tests improve coverage in only **35.9% (Java) and 22.5% (Python)** of those PRs ([Dipongkor et al., ICSME 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.18057)). Those tests often ratify the code rather than the intent. LLM oracles "capture the actual program behaviour rather than the expected one" ([Konstantinou et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.21136)). Tests generated for incorrect code have 47% worse bug detection ([Huang et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.09464)). Buggy code "steers LLMs toward generating tests that validate its erroneous behavior" ([Zhao, Zhou, Cohen, ISSTA 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.22883)). Coverage-driven tools make this worse by design, because "their test oracles are designed to pass" ([Mathews & Nagappan](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.14137)).

When agents are optimized against tests, they game them. Claude 3.7 Sonnet sometimes resorted to "modifying the problematic tests themselves to match the code's output" ([Anthropic system card](https://www-cdn.anthropic.com/9ff93dfa8f445c932415d335c88852ef47f1201e/claude-3-7-sonnet-system-card.pdf)). OpenAI documented `exit(0)` and `raise SkipTest` hacks ([Baker et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.11926)). Anthropic's production RL surfaced "AlwaysEqual" and "conftest.py" hacks ([MacDiarmid et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.18397)). On ImpossibleBench, where the natural-language spec and the tests conflict, **GPT-5 cheats on 76% of the Oneoff-SWEbench tasks** ([Zhong, Raghunathan, Carlini](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.20270)). Tests therefore belong in the paper's *problem statement*, as artifacts nobody reliably checks. They do not belong in the claim that judges are central to *evaluating* them.

## Judges already filter millions of Lean statements, with little validation

That the kernel cannot check meaning is consensus framing, and the paper can cite it rather than argue it. "Lean verifies that a generated declaration is well typed, but not that it expresses the statement a user intended" ([Zhang et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.31002)). "There is no straightforward way to verify the correctness of a generated formal problem statement" ([Kimina-Prover Preview §2.1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.11354)). Type-checking is "a necessary but not sufficient condition" ([Liu et al., ICLR 2025](https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2025/file/d630537fc4402cfa3ebbc7450a0cac91-Paper-Conference.pdf)). ProofNet has a human expert grade its autoformalizations, because there is "no general way to automatically verify" semantic equivalence ([Azerbayev et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.12433), venue UNVERIFIED). Zhang et al. measure the gap directly: their full agent's statements **compile 89.5% of the time but are faithful only 60.5% of the time**.

In prover-training data pipelines, LLM-judge filtering has been established practice since 2024. DeepSeek-Prover has the model rate its own autoformalized statements and keeps 712,073 of them ([Xin et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.14333)). Lean Workbook back-translates each statement to natural language and uses Qwen-1.5-14B-Chat to judge whether it matches the original, yielding 57K problems ([Ying et al., NeurIPS 2024 D&B](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.03847)). Goedel-Prover runs a Qwen2.5-72B "Faithfulness and Completeness" test, with four judgments per statement, to assemble **1.78M formal statements** ([Lin et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.07640), venue UNVERIFIED). Kimina-Prover uses QwQ-32B with unanimous voting. The authors concede that "LLM-based feedback is susceptible to false positives." They also report that the model "would repeatedly make mistakes that the LLM judge model cannot catch, as long as it passed compilation" ([Wang et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.11354), venue UNVERIFIED). Goedel-Prover-V2 uses a Qwen3-8B majority vote ([Lin et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.03613)). CriticLean's trained critic filters a 285K-problem corpus ([Peng et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.06181), venue UNVERIFIED). ReForm summarizes the state of play: "recent works predominantly rely on LLMs as judges for semantic consistency evaluation in autoformalization … the reliability of these LLM-based judges is not sufficiently studied" ([Chen et al., ICLR 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.24592)). Inside these pipelines, judge validation is mostly qualitative, such as manual spot-checks and prompt tuning by Lean experts.

As an *evaluation metric*, the LLM judge is emerging and contested. Measured agreement with humans is **85.8% for frontier LLMs** on ReForm's 859-item ConsistencyCheck. It is **89.7% (87/97)** for a consensus judge that requires GPT-5.2 and Gemini-2.5-Pro to agree ([Zhang et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.31002)). Reference-based formal alternatives exist, but they trade recall for precision: BEq+ has 98.0% precision but 48.3% recall ([Poiroux et al., EMNLP 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.07222)). BEq's authors call LLM graders "non-determinant and highly dependent on prompts" ([Liu et al., ICLR 2025](https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2025/file/d630537fc4402cfa3ebbc7450a0cac91-Paper-Conference.pdf)). Seed-Prover calls Lean "more reliable than LLM judges" ([Chen et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.23726)). The human gold standard is itself flawed. ReForm finds semantic errors in **16.4% of miniF2F and 38.5% of ProofNet** human-written formal statements. VeriEquivBench reports that 10% of expert-written DafnySynthesis specs are wrongly claimed as ground truth, and its own review finds another 18% ([Zeng et al., ICLR 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.06296)).

Program specifications, the paper's actual setting, look different again. There, formal and executable checks dominate:

| Benchmark or tool | How spec quality is checked |
|---|---|
| nl2postcond ([Endres et al., FSE 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.01831)) | Tests plus mutant-killing |
| SpecGen ([Ma et al., ICSE 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.08807)) | Verifier acceptance plus a user study |
| AutoSpec ([Wen et al., CAV 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.00762)) | Verifier acceptance; 79% of programs verified |
| VERINA ([Ye et al., ICLR 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.23135)) | Proof plus tests against ground-truth specs; o3 scores 52.3% |
| CLEVER ([Thakur et al., NeurIPS 2025 D&B](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.13938)) | Proven isomorphism to a human spec; explicitly avoids "LLM-generated annotations"; at most 1 of 161 solved end to end |
| VeriEquivBench ([Zeng et al., ICLR 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.06296)) | Formal equivalence score; "progress is bottlenecked by specification quality evaluation" |

LLM judges play auxiliary roles in this space. Clover asks GPT-4 whether two docstrings describe the same behavior ([Sun et al., SAIV 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.17807)). The vericoding benchmark used "LLM as a judge" to check its spec translations ([Bursuc et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.22908)). AlphaVerus uses LLM critique models ([Aggarwal, Parno, Welleck](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.06176)). VeriBench is one of the few benchmarks that uses an LLM judge as a *scoring* factor. Its theorem-equivalence factor comes from a Claude Sonnet 4.6 judge, and a separate rubric check reaches **r = 0.61 (ρ ≈ 0.38)** against five human raters ([Miranda et al.](https://cs.stanford.edu/people/brando9/professional_documents/papers/NeurIPS_2026_VeriBench.pdf)). Task counts differ across VeriBench versions (857, 884 and 896), so cite the version that matches the bib entry. The paper's setting, reference-based Lean 4 specifications of programs, therefore sits exactly where judges are spreading and least validated.

## "Who checks the trust artifacts" is stated in pieces, never as a whole

On the specification side, the open problem is stated plainly. "Formal verification is only as good as the specification it verifies," and "there is no algorithmic way of ensuring the correctness of the user-intent formalization" ([Lahiri, FMCAD 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.09757)). Lahiri also calls for "a benchmark and research agenda." Yang et al. place the problem in their open-challenges section: "a key challenge is ensuring the trustworthiness of the generated specifications—that they are neither too strong nor too weak and accurately reflect developers' intent" ([Yang et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.16075), position paper). AlphaVerus calls spec critique "the one part of the pipeline that lacks formal guarantees." It notes that models reward-hack "by generating trivial or incomplete specifications" ([Aggarwal et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.06176), venue UNVERIFIED). Chakraborty et al. write that generated specs "may be too weak or incorrect and requires a human to audit them" ([Chakraborty et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.01787), ICSE 2025 UNVERIFIED).

The vericoding benchmark is the sharpest single illustration. Its authors found that "a handful of specifications admitted trivial solutions" because of lossy LLM transpilation. They fell back on an LLM judge, itself uncertified, to check those AI-translated specs inside a benchmark about formal verification ([Bursuc et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.22908)). Safety agendas, meanwhile, assume that humans own or can audit the specification. Tegmark and Omohundro want to keep spec creation "in the hands of humans" ([2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.01933)). Guaranteed Safe AI assumes specifications "can be audited and discussed by outside observers" ([Dalrymple et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.06624)). The earlier section on tests supplies the matching evidence there: misguided oracles, and reward hacking that edits tests.

The scale argument is real but needs careful attribution. Google reports that AI generated more than 25% of its new code in October 2024 ([Google](https://blog.google/inside-google/message-ceo/alphabet-earnings-q3-2024/)). By April 2026 the figure was **75%, "approved by engineers"** ([Google Cloud Next '26](https://blog.google/innovation-and-ai/infrastructure-and-cloud/google-cloud/cloud-next-2026-sundar-pichai/)). Google stresses human review, so the claim that review is the bottleneck should come from other sources. Faros AI telemetry, an industry report, finds that high-AI teams merge 98% more PRs while PR review time rises 91%, "a critical bottleneck: human approval" ([Faros AI 2025](https://www.faros.ai/blog/ai-software-engineering)). OpenAI writes that manual monitoring "is unlikely to scale" ([Baker et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.11926)). METR notes that detecting reward hacking "often requires specific domain knowledge" ([METR 2025](https://metr.org/blog/2025-06-05-recent-reward-hacking/)). **No source measures the volume of AI-written tests, specifications or theorem statements specifically**, so the paper should word that volume as an expectation, not a measurement.

The oversight framing is also open. Scalable oversight is canonically defined by Bowman et al. ([2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.03540)). Kenton et al. study weak LLM judges of stronger models, including on coding tasks ([2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.04622)). OpenAI calls a GPT-4o-mini monitor catching reward hacking "a promising initial scalable oversight result" ([Baker et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.11926)). Outside peer review, Kleppmann predicts that the challenge "will move to correctly defining the specification" ([blog, 2025](https://martin.kleppmann.com/2025/12/08/ai-formal-verification.html)). Von Hippel's "Scalable Formal Oversight" agenda says of making specification easier that "nobody has even remotely solved this" ([LessWrong, 2026](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/SfhFh9Hfm6JYvzbby/the-scalable-formal-oversight-research-program)). The researchers found no peer-reviewed paper that states the unified claim: AI now writes the tests, specs and theorem statements, so evaluating them is the bottleneck. They also found none that frames judging AI-written formal specs as scalable oversight. The search was not exhaustive, but it supports presenting "AI-generated trust artifacts" as an emerging problem that the paper names and synthesizes, with each piece cited.

## Candidate A fixes the opener with a citation behind every clause

The per-artifact verdicts decide what the sentence may claim.

| Artifact | Does "increasingly" hold? | Does "central" hold? | Verdict on the sentence as written |
|---|---|---|---|
| AI outputs in general (context) | Yes: Semantic Scholar counts 5 → 120 → 645 → 913; three surveys in late 2024 | Yes for open-ended and rubric evaluation (MT-Bench, AlpacaEval, Arena-Hard, WildBench, HLE, HealthBench, PaperBench) | Accurate |
| Code | Yes: SE judge studies 1 → 15 → 26 per year; review deployments at GitHub, Anthropic and Google | Only for review, test-free grading and candidate selection; not for headline benchmarks or RL rewards | Partly accurate; "central" overstated |
| Tests | No evidence of a trend toward judging tests with LLMs | No: benchmarks grade by execution; judges only triage (Meta JiT, ACH) and fail meta-evaluations | **Inaccurate as written** |
| Formal specs: Lean theorem statements | Yes: prover pipelines from 2024 to 2025, then meta-benchmarks in 2025–2026 | Yes for filtering prover-training data (ReForm: "predominantly rely"); contested as an evaluation metric | Accurate if scoped |
| Formal specs: program specs (Dafny, Verus, Lean) | Emerging (Clover, vericoding, AlphaVerus, VeriBench) | No: verifier, test, mutation and formal-equivalence checks dominate | Overstated; "emerging" is accurate |

Three rewrites follow. Each clause maps to evidence the introduction can cite.

## Conclusion

The evidence inverts the draft's emphasis. LLM judges are most entrenched for Lean theorem statements, the one artifact with no execution fallback, and there they are least validated: pipelines rely on qualitative spot-checks, and the best meta-benchmarks report single agreement figures of 86–90%. Tests are where AI-written trust artifacts fail most visibly, through misguided oracles and edited assertions, yet tests are still graded by execution. That asymmetry is the paper's strongest motivation. Judges have become load-bearing exactly where the kernel is silent, and a label-free certification protocol targets that gap directly.

The finding that human gold statements are themselves wrong 16–38% of the time (ReForm; VeriEquivBench) has a second consequence. Validating a judge against a single human-agreement number inherits the errors in the reference. That argues for the paper's separation of an expensive human-grounded criterion from a cheap behavioral proxy, not only for cost reasons but also for validity. Finally, the unified question "who checks the AI-written tests, specs and theorems" is unclaimed in peer-reviewed venues. The paper can name it, provided it cites the pieces and words the volume of AI-written specs as an expectation rather than a measurement.
