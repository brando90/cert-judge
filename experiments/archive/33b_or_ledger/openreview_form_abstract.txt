When AI agents write their own trust artifacts, such as tests and formal specifications that establish trust in their code, those artifacts need checking at scale. In end-to-end autoformalization, for instance, agents choose their own interfaces and theorem statements, so correct answers take many forms and the reference's checks fit none. A trustworthy AI judge must then assess such trust artifacts, and every evaluation built on it inherits its errors. The standard evidence that such a judge can be trusted is agreement with human ratings, a check repeated for every new model, prompt, and task. We propose instead to collect labels once and screen every new judge with falsifiable properties that fix how scores must respond to controlled edits. Four properties target identity, bug monotonicity, missing-specification monotonicity, and stability for Lean 4 specifications scored against a gold reference. A fixed equal-weight index, $TI_{var}$, is the geometric mean of bug monotonicity and two variance penalties. The geometric mean acts as a smooth conjunction, so no strong component can offset a weak one. On a 72-pair validation set, the index tracks judge-level human agreement across three model families with eight configurations each. For the Codex panel, the Spearman correlation is 0.833 on averaged labels and 0.905 and 0.738 on the two rating passes. Bug-ladder checks expose a deliberately lexical judge that matches the semantic prompts' human agreement yet reverses on 20 of 24 ladders. Judge validation thus moves from a per-judge annotation cost toward a one-time human panel plus automated, falsifiable checks that any new judge must pass.
