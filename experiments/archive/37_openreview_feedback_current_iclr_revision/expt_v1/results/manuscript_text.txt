009                                               A BSTRACT
010
011            When AI agents write their own trust artifacts, such as tests and formal specifica-
012            tions that establish trust in their code, those artifacts need checking at scale. In
013            end-to-end autoformalization, for instance, agents choose their own interfaces and
014            theorem statements, so correct answers take many forms and the reference’s checks
015            fit none. A trustworthy AI judge must then assess such trust artifacts, and every
016            evaluation built on it inherits its errors. The standard evidence that such a judge can
017            be trusted is agreement with human ratings, a check repeated for every new model,
018
               prompt, and task. We propose instead to collect labels once and screen every new
               judge with falsifiable properties that fix how scores must respond to controlled edits.
019
               Four properties target identity, bug monotonicity, missing-specification monotonic-
020
               ity, and stability for Lean 4 specifications scored against a gold reference. A fixed
021            equal-weight index, TIvar , is the geometric mean of bug monotonicity and two
022            variance penalties. The geometric mean acts as a smooth conjunction, so no strong
023            component can offset a weak one. On a 72-pair validation set, the index tracks
024            judge-level human agreement across three model families with eight configura-
025            tions each. For the Codex panel, the Spearman correlation is 0.833 on averaged
026            labels and 0.905 and 0.738 on the two rating passes. Bug-ladder checks expose a
027            deliberately lexical judge that matches the semantic prompts’ human agreement
028            yet reverses on 20 of 24 ladders. Judge validation thus moves from a per-judge
029
               annotation cost toward a one-time human panel plus automated, falsifiable checks
               that any new judge must pass.

      1   I NTRODUCTION
033
034
      AI agents now write the trust artifacts for their own code: the tests (Haque et al., 2026) and formal
035
      specifications (Yang et al., 2024) on which trust in that code rests. A formal specification states the
036   guarantees a program should satisfy, such as returning a sorted permutation of its input. To trust an
037   AI-written trust artifact, one must verify the artifact and verify whatever judge performs that check.
038   We start with formal specifications because the proof assistant fixes what a theorem means, so the
039   remaining question is whether it captures the intent.
040
      In end-to-end autoformalization, an agent receives source code and writes the Lean 4 implementation,
041
      tests, theorem statements, and proofs without a scaffold (Miranda et al., 2026). Correct solutions take
042   many forms, since none need share the reference’s interfaces, data representations, or decomposition
043   into theorem statements. Two questions then arise: whether the candidate’s proofs establish its own
044   theorems, and whether those theorems express the reference’s guarantees. Lean settles the first, but a
045   proof does not establish that the theorems it proves cover the reference’s guarantees (Lahiri, 2024).
046   Rerunning the reference’s tests and theorems cannot settle the second, because they fail against a
047   different representation regardless of correctness. Translating code into another language raises the
048   same mismatch whenever the translation chooses its own interfaces. What remains is a comparison of
049   meaning between the candidate’s and the reference’s specifications. Large language models (LLMs)
050
      already serve as that judge (Miranda et al., 2026).
051   Prior work in AI addresses the first verification with an AI judge and the second with human agreement.
052   LLM judges filter autoformalized statements in theorem-proving pipelines (Lin et al., 2025; Wang
053   et al., 2025) and score agent-written specifications against references (Miranda et al., 2026). A judge
      counts as trustworthy once its scores agree with expert annotations (Chen et al., 2026) or with human

054
      ratings under a rubric (Liu et al., 2023; Zheng et al., 2023). On objective tasks, consistency across
055   repeated judgments serves as further evidence (Schroeder & Wood-Doughty, 2024). Agreement
056   varies across models, tasks, and evaluated properties, so evidence for one configuration does not
057   establish another’s reliability (Bavaresco et al., 2025). Existing labels can assess another judge on
058   the same examples without fresh annotation. However, repeatedly selecting judges against those
059   labels risks adaptive overfitting (Dwork et al., 2015). New artifact distributions also raise a separate
060   question about whether those labels remain representative.
061   We propose using one human panel to assess a property suite, then screening subsequent judge
062   configurations without consulting those labels. The screen asks whether a judge responds appropri-
063   ately when a controlled edit preserves or damages a reference artifact. A failed property identifies a
064   concrete behavior that human agreement alone can hide. Passing the screen supplies evidence about
065   the tested behaviors, without guaranteeing agreement on unseen artifacts.
066
      We instantiate this protocol for Lean 4 formal specifications judged against gold references (Figure 1).
067   Four properties test identity, responses to cumulative bugs, responses to removed theorem statements
068   and unit tests, and score stability. A fixed equal-weight index, TIvar , summarizes bug monotonicity
069   and two variance penalties. The full diagnostic suite remains necessary because this scalar omits
070   some properties.
071
      On a 72-pair validation set, the index tracks judge-level human agreement across three families, each
072
      containing eight configurations. The Codex association is Spearman 0.833, with a 95% bootstrap
073   interval of [0.24, 1.00]. These estimates are optimistic because the same labels informed the index
074   form, prompts, aggregation, and threshold. A deliberately lexical judge agrees with humans yet
075   reverses its scores on most bug ladders.
076
      The aim is to amortize annotation through reusable checks, with transfer to new judges and domains
077
      assessed separately. Our contributions are:
078
079
             • A protocol that combines a one-time human validation set with four falsifiable properties for
080
               screening judge configurations without new labels (Section 4).
081
082          • An evaluation linking committed scores to validation-set association, lexical failures, and
083            the limits of reducing the property suite to one index (Section 5.3).

086   2    R ELATED W ORK
087
088   LLM-as-a-judge validation. G-E VAL (Liu et al., 2023) and MT-B ENCH (Zheng et al., 2023)
089   compare judges with human ratings, leaving reliability dependent on the evaluated tasks and config-
090   urations. Bavaresco et al. (2025) document this dependence across tasks rather than establishing a
091   universal judge. Schroeder & Wood-Doughty (2024) measure consistency across repeated judgments,
092   which does not by itself establish correctness. Our protocol retains human agreement as a validation
093   criterion and adds tests with specified directions of response.
094
095   Judges for code. ICE-S CORE (Zhuo, 2024) and C ODE J UDGE (Tong & Zhang, 2024) use models to
096   assess generated code where execution tests provide incomplete evidence, but their human-agreement
097   results do not certify later judge configurations. SWT-B ENCH validates generated tests against
098   developers’ fixes (Mündler et al., 2024), requiring a reference behavior rather than treating test
099   execution as proof of test validity. Our controlled perturbations serve a related purpose for judges:
100   they specify how a score should change without labeling each perturbed artifact.
101
102   Judging formal statements. F ORMAL A LIGN learns alignment between informal and formal state-
103   ments (Lu et al., 2025), whereas our target is the behavior of a judge comparing candidate and gold
104   specifications. R E F ORM validates formal-statement judges against expert annotations (Chen et al.,
105   2026), without establishing that later configurations satisfy our behavioral properties. V ERI B ENCH
106   evaluates agent-generated Lean artifacts (Miranda et al., 2026); we study the judges used to score
107   their formal specifications. These tasks require checking what a statement means, beyond whether a
      proof checker accepts it.

      3    P ROBLEM S ETUP
122
123   Intent, specification, and artifact. An item contains an informal problem statement, a gold Lean 4
124
      artifact g, and a candidate artifact x generated by an agent. We call the informal requirement the intent.
      An artifact is a Lean source file containing an implementation and, potentially, theorem statements,
125
      predicates, unit tests, and proofs. Its formal specification consists of the theorem statements about
126
      the implementation together with the predicates they use. For example, a sorting specification can
127   require both ordered output and preservation of the input elements. A proof certifies a theorem under
128   its stated assumptions; it does not determine whether those requirements capture the intent. A unit
129   test checks a concrete case and is not part of the specification under our terminology (Appendix F).
130   We use “specification” in this sense regardless of who wrote it.
131
132   Reference-based specification quality. The target is how completely and correctly the candidate
133   specification captures the gold reference. A candidate can type-check yet omit a requirement, impose
134   an unintended assumption, or assert a vacuous property. The judge prompts ask which reference
135   guarantees the candidate captures and assign a score of zero to trivial statements such as True
136   (Appendix A). This scoring instruction does not establish that judges detect every such failure. A
137   gold reference supplies a concrete comparison target; it does not make the judge immune to strategic
138   manipulation (Robertson & Koyejo, 2025).
139   With aligned interfaces or a specified correspondence between representations, symbolic proof
140   attempts can check implications between candidate and reference statements. The symbolic checker
141   BEq+ attempts both directions and reports both false positives and false negatives relative to human
142   judgments (Poiroux et al., 2025). A successful proof establishes the encoded implication under its
143   assumptions, not faithful intent. Unsuccessful proof search does not establish inequivalence. We did
144   not benchmark such a tool against the graded reference-coverage judgments studied here.
145
146   Judge configuration. We define θ = (model, prompt, nrepeats , aggregation) and write its score as
147   Jθ (x, g) ∈ [0, 1]. The headline panel uses a median over repeated calls. Despite the prompt label
148   “REFERENCE IMPLEMENTATION,” its reference field contains the gold file’s final theorem block;
149   its candidate field contains the full candidate file. Thus the measured task depends on this extraction
150
      rule, not only on the nominal rubric. A change to that rule requires a new assessment. We distinguish
      a judge’s agreement with artifact ratings from the association, across judge configurations, between
151
      that agreement and a property index.

155
156   A one-time panel and reusable checks. Human ratings link judge behavior to the intended scoring
157   task on a fixed validation set. For each configuration, we measure agreement with those ratings and
158   evaluate scripted perturbations of gold artifacts. The perturbations require judge calls but no additional
159   human labels. We use the validation set to select the index form, prompts, repeat aggregation, and
160   decision threshold. After freezing these choices, subsequent configurations can face the same property
161   checks without accessing the labels (Figure 1). This is a screening protocol; the present evidence
      does not establish transfer to an unseen configuration or distribution.

177   Four falsifiable properties. P1 (identity) requires the maximum score when an artifact is compared
178   with itself, Jθ (g, g) = 1. In the three primary families and Leanstral, the identity summary uses
179   four rewrite types; unedited self-comparisons appear at bug-ladder level 0 (Appendix E). P2 (bug
180   monotonicity) requires non-increasing scores as errors accumulate. The historical bug ladder used
181   here alters the candidate text, including theorem statements; it is not restricted to implementation
182   bugs. P3 (missing-specification monotonicity) requires non-increasing scores as theorem statements
183   and unit tests are removed. We retain this historical property name although unit tests are not
184
      specifications under Section 3. Each ladder extends the preceding level, so its expected ordering
      concerns cumulative damage rather than equal severity of individual edits. Ties are allowed; a local
185
      score increase is a reversal. P4 (stability) requires stable scores across repeated calls and meaning-
186
      preserving rewrites. These include alpha-renaming, which consistently changes bound-variable names
187   without changing their meaning. The properties are targets for the probes; they are not theorems
188   establishing the validity of every scripted transformation.
189
190   A fixed equal-weight summary. The protocol retains individual diagnostics because different
191   failures need different checks. Our primary scalar, TIvar , uses only bug-ladder scores, combining
192   their average monotonicity with two variance penalties:

      The geometric mean acts as a smooth conjunction: a strong component cannot fully offset a weak
201
      one, and equal weights avoid fitting component weights to the human ratings. The scalar nevertheless
202
      omits P1, P3, rewrite invariance, and a direct penalty for local reversals. It therefore summarizes

204   means that its absolute value can change when the comparison panel changes. Using it prospectively
205   requires a frozen normalization reference, whose transfer we have not tested.

      panel’s holistic ratings. Its Spearman agreement with panel labels is approximately 0.13 on 63 shared
399
      pairs.
400
401   Two pre-registered screens asked whether property indices tracked agreement with that pool. The
402   screen of 14 configurations was underpowered; the expanded screen of 32 found no index meeting
403
      its registered criterion. Its largest absolute Spearman association was −0.39 with Holm-adjusted
      permutation p = 0.25; leave-one-out selection transfer yielded ρ = 0.24, p = 0.49. These negative
404
      results do not demonstrate transfer under the original criterion, nor can the pool serve as a test set for
405
      that criterion. Appendix D.3 records the corresponding negative-control evidence.
406
407
408   Coverage of the screen. The probes do not establish sensitivity to vacuity, subtle weakening,
409   over-constraint, or formatting-only edits beyond the four rewrites studied here. Constructing probes
410   with known directions for these failures is the next methodological step; we claim no result for
411   them. The language-model configurations use single-call rubric prompts per repeat; structured
412   multi-stage judging is untested. The historical alpha generator can produce malformed text, so its low
413   scores do not establish sensitivity to valid alpha-renaming. Other scripted rewrites and the intended
414   ordering of bug edits also lack semantic validation. We cannot separate rubric ambiguity from
415
      misunderstanding Lean semantics with the current annotations. Presentation, including redundant
      theorems, implementation bloat, and unnecessary abstraction, is unassessed; holistic human ratings
416
      may reflect it implicitly. We did not optimize candidates against the judge, so resistance to gaming is
417
      untested.
418
419   All candidates and probes come from V ERI B ENCH in Lean 4. The protocol could be instantiated for
420   another proof assistant, but neither cross-assistant nor cross-benchmark transfer has been tested. A
421
      score is relative to the supplied gold reference and extraction rule, not a guarantee that all intended
      requirements are present. Reference-free screening and the number of annotators needed for a stable
422
      criterion remain open. The index also depends on its comparison panel and omits constant ladders
423
      with undefined correlations; these implementation choices limit portable screening.
424
425
426   A locked test. A decisive test would first freeze the rubric, property generators, score extraction,
427   aggregation, index, normalization reference, and any rejection rule. Independent raters would
428   then label new candidate–gold pairs under that rubric, with new judge configurations fixed before
429   their labels are examined. The analysis would report both artifact-level agreement and judge-level
430   association, including uncertainty and property-specific failures. A separate distribution-shift test
431   would be needed for new domains. Because the original rubric text is unavailable, recovering it or
      validating an explicitly reconstructed criterion is a prerequisite to claiming a matched-rubric test.

      7   C ONCLUSION
433
434
      A one-time human panel can support a protocol that screens new judges through falsifiable behavioral
435   checks without collecting labels for each configuration. On the present validation set, the fixed
436   equal-weight index is associated with human agreement, but its high scores can coexist with lexical
437   failures. Individual property diagnostics therefore remain essential, and a locked test must establish
438   transfer beyond the labels used to select the protocol. The goal is to make human judgment a reusable
439   foundation for checking new judges, with each judge accountable to explicit tests of its behavior.

960   F      U NIT T ESTS AS G ROUND T HEOREMS : A D EFINITION W E D O N OT A DOPT
961
962   Section 3 follows common formal-methods usage: the formal specification consists of theorem
963   statements about the implementation, and unit tests are not part of it. We record a competing view that
964   we do not adopt. In Lean 4, a unit test is often itself a closed theorem. The gold myAdd artifact states
965   its tests as, e.g., example : myAdd 1 2 = 3 := by native_decide, an anonymous
966   theorem with no free variables. On this view, a unit test specifies the implementation at one concrete
967   input, and specifications range from such ground instances to universally quantified properties such
968   as ∀n, myAdd n 0 = n. The choice changes terminology, not our measurements. The P3 ladder
969   removes theorem statements and unit tests alike; under the adopted definition, only the removed
970
      theorems count as missing specification.
