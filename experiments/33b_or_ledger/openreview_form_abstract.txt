Large language models judge whether AI-written formal specifications express the right guarantees, a question that running tests or checking proofs cannot settle. Practitioners establish each judge's reliability through agreement with human ratings and repeat that check for every new model, prompt, and domain. We propose collecting labels once, then screening new judges with falsifiable properties that specify how scores should respond to controlled edits. Four properties target identity, bug monotonicity, missing-specification monotonicity, and stability for Lean 4 specifications. A fixed equal-weight index, $TI_{var}$, combines bug monotonicity with two variance penalties multiplicatively, so no strong component can offset a weak one. On a 72-pair validation set, the index tracks judge-level human agreement across three model families with eight configurations each. For the Codex panel, the Spearman correlation is 0.833 on averaged labels and 0.905 and 0.738 on the two rating passes. Bug-ladder checks expose a deliberately lexical judge that matches the semantic prompts' human agreement yet reverses on 20 of 24 ladders. Judge validation thus moves from a per-judge annotation cost toward a one-time human panel plus automated, falsifiable checks that any new judge must pass.
