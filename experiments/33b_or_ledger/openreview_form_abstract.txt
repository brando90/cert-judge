Large language models judge whether AI-written formal specifications express the right guarantees, a question that running tests or checking proofs cannot settle. Reliability is usually assessed through agreement with human ratings, leaving each new judge configuration dependent on human validation. We propose collecting labels once, then screening new judges with falsifiable properties that specify how scores should respond to controlled edits. Four properties target identity, bug monotonicity, missing-specification monotonicity, and stability for Lean 4 specifications. A fixed equal-weight index, $TI_{var}$, summarizes bug monotonicity and variance. We compare three judge families with eight configurations each on a 72-pair, single-annotator validation set that also informed protocol selection. For Codex, index--human-agreement Spearman correlations are 0.833 on averaged labels and 0.905 and 0.738 on the two rating passes. Bug-ladder reversals expose a lexical grader that human agreement alone would favor, although the scalar index still scores it highly. Judge validation thus moves toward a one-time human panel plus automated, falsifiable checks that each new judge must face.
